## Introduction

Welcome to the Forever Class Codex. This is an audio walkthrough of how World of Warcraft Forever changes the nine Alliance classes, compared with the Vanilla and Classic versions you already know.

This is aimed at veterans. I'm going to assume you know what a stance dance is, why Paladins couldn't taunt, and what powershifting meant for a Feral Druid. The goal is not to explain the classes. The goal is to tell you what's different, and which class is worth rolling if you want to feel those differences.

Everything here reflects the beta as of October twenty-second, twenty twenty-six. Launch is November fourth. The last build that changed classes was the October sixteenth build, and everything you'll hear includes those late changes. Treat it all as launch-candidate. Blizzard has said more than once that talents and abilities can keep moving until release.

Here's the frame for the whole episode. Forever keeps the Classic skeleton. Outdoor mobs are still meant to take ten to fifteen seconds to kill. Threat, crowd control, mana, weapon swings and positioning all still matter. What Blizzard changed are the places where Vanilla classes were unfinished. Specs that couldn't do their advertised job. Resource loops that broke. Talents you had to buy just to make the class work. Most of the new abilities give you one more meaningful decision every few seconds, or a real capstone to build around. They don't turn the game into modern rotational WoW.

## System changes

Before the classes, a few system changes that touch everyone.

First, talents. The trees are still Classic trees, with rows that unlock as you go and a thirty-one point capstone. But there's a new milestone at sixteen points, alongside the familiar eleven, twenty-one and thirty-one. Blizzard calls it the gold medal tier, and a lot of build-defining talents sit there. Talents that every player was forced to buy just to make the class function have become baseline. Divine Spirit. Blessing of Kings. Improved Mark of the Wild style effects. That frees up points for real choices.

Second, dual specialization arrives at level forty. For hybrids, that's enormous. Paladins, Priests, Druids and Shamans no longer pay a respec tax to keep a healing set and a questing set. If you like two specs of the same class, that's now a great reason to roll it.

Third, hit and crit are merged. There's no longer a separate physical and spell version of each. Caster weapons carry real spell power. And healing power counts as one third damage, so your healing gear no longer makes solo play miserable.

Fourth, downranking is curtailed. Spells far below your level get much less benefit from spell power, and lower proc chances. Blizzard's own example is a level sixty Mage spamming rank one Frostbolt, which no longer gets normal Frostbite behavior. Where the old trick did something useful, they've added a real spell instead. We'll hear about the Mage's Frost Blast later.

Fifth, raid buffs are much less tied to group composition. Late beta made Battle Shout raid-wide. Trueshot Aura, Moonkin Aura and Leader of the Pack are raid-wide. The Shaman totems Grace of Air, Strength of Earth, Windfury and Flametongue are all raid-wide. And Blessing of Salvation no longer competes with your other Blessings. The old puzzle of arranging five-player groups around buffs is mostly gone.

Two Alliance-specific notes. Dwarf Shaman is now an Alliance option. Human Hunter and Gnome Priest are new combinations too. And the new Skyborne race can be Warriors, Hunters, Rogues, Druids and Mages. Also, weapon-skill racials are gone. Human Sword Specialization became Ruthless, which is two percent crit. Dwarf Mace Specialization became Earth Fury, which is two percent critical damage and healing. So Human is no longer the automatic melee race just because of swords.

Now, the classes. I'll go in the classic order, and for each one I'll tell you how far it has moved from Vanilla.

## Warrior

Warrior. Distance from Vanilla: low to moderate. This is the safest pick if you want Classic, just better. Of all nine classes, Warrior changes the least in concept.

The biggest change is rage generation. Rage from taking damage is now calculated before armor and absorbs. So better gear, or a Priest shield on you, no longer starves your resource. Offensive rage is normalized much more tightly around weapon speed, and critical hits generate extra. Rage gets more predictable, but you still manage it.

Tactical Mastery is baseline at level fourteen, letting you keep ten rage through a stance change. Stance dancing stays. You just don't spend deep Arms points to make it pleasant. Victory Rush arrives at level twenty and heals you for ten percent of your health after a kill, so a low health bar no longer kills your leveling momentum.

Recklessness, Retaliation and Shield Wall no longer share a cooldown, and Retaliation and Shield Wall drop to fifteen minutes. Rend and Thunder Clap scale with attack power, Thunder Clap works in Defensive Stance, and Sunder Armor threat scales with attack power. Late beta also made Battle Shout raid-wide, and made Slam immune to spell pushback.

Arms gets a talent called Weaponmaster, which merges the old sword, axe and mace specializations. Axes and polearms favor crit. Maces and staves get armor penetration. Swords keep their extra attacks. No more weapon-spec lottery when your raid drop is the wrong type. Spearing Strike sits at the sixteen point milestone. Bloodthrill lets Rend enable Overpower. And Improved Slam no longer delays or interrupts your auto attack. Arms becomes a deliberate weapon master spec built around Overpower, Rend and Slam, instead of a wait for Mortal Strike.

Fury's sixteen point talent is Raging Blows, which improves Whirlwind with two weapons and makes Cleave cheaper. Off-hand hit is less of a problem, Enrage can trigger from taking damage, and Death Wish now costs you extra damage taken instead of armor. Bloodthirst loses its tiny heal and grants movement speed instead.

Protection is still sword and board, with cleaner plumbing. More rage from blocks. Master of Defense generates rage when you avoid attacks with your shield. Vanguard lets you Charge into Defensive Stance play. Focused Rage trims ability costs. Shield Slam scales better with block value. And Improved Shield Block and Improved Taunt move toward baseline.

The verdict: choose Warrior when you specifically don't want Forever to feel like another expansion. You still manage rage, stances, swings and threat. Forever mostly removes the places where Vanilla Warrior fought its own mechanics.

## Paladin

Paladin. Distance from Vanilla: very high. This is one of the biggest overhauls in the game. Blizzard's stated goal is that a Paladin can be a real healer, a real tank, or a real Retribution damage dealer.

Two baseline changes set the tone. Every Paladin learns Holy Strike early, an instant Holy weapon attack. And Judgment no longer consumes your Seal. That single change removes the judge and reseal routine that made Vanilla Paladin feel so passive.

Consecration is baseline at level twenty instead of a Holy talent. Only the first four enemies take full damage and threat, so it's not an unlimited area bomb. Late beta lowered its spell power coefficient significantly, and raised Righteous Fury's Holy threat from sixty percent to ninety percent. Blizzard is steering Protection toward deliberate tank threat, not a scaling area spell.

Reckoning keeps its Classic behavior. It stores up to four charges and releases them on your next auto attack.

Protection is built around a new seal, Seal of Fury. It adds Holy damage, favors fast weapons, and turns some of that into absorb shields while you wear a shield. And its Judgment taunts. Alliance Paladins finally have a real taunt. The talents build around it. The seal's mitigation returns mana. Swift Judgment recovers Judgment faster. Iron Creed ties Holy Strike to mitigation. And Templar's Bulwark is a big absorb cooldown based on your maximum health. Put it together and Prot has a tank seal, a taunt, baseline Consecration, stronger threat, real mitigation, mana sustain and a serious defensive cooldown. Five-man groups no longer have to work around a missing taunt.

Retribution gets much more active. Sacred Arbiter, the sixteen point talent, empowers Holy Strike and refreshes your Judgments. Champion of the Light converts Intellect into spell damage, though late beta cut it from twenty, forty and sixty percent down to ten, twenty and thirty. Vindication lowers the enemy's attack power and raises yours. Instrument of Law improves Hammer of Wrath. And then there's Twist of Light, which turns seal twisting into a talent. Your previous seal echoes into your next melee hit. Ret keeps seal twisting, but you learn it from the tree instead of a swing timer guide.

Holy has the smallest role change, but much more spell choice. Voice of Truth helps you resist silences and interrupts. Reverence improves in-combat mana regeneration from Spirit. Infusion of Light makes Holy Shock and Flash of Light crits speed up your next Holy Light. Divine Favor moves to the sixteen point tier, and Holy Shock sits deeper with a much shorter cooldown than in Vanilla. The capstone, Light's Vigil, resets Holy Shock and then pays off with damage or a party heal, depending on the target. Holy now runs on a Holy Shock engine instead of rank-picked Flash of Light spam.

The verdict: if you liked the idea of Vanilla Paladin, but not what happened when you tried to tank or play Ret at sixty, this is the clearest play-it-again pick in Forever. It's still slow, mana-hungry, seal and judgment Paladin. It just finally has enough machinery in all three specs.

## Hunter

Hunter. Distance from Vanilla: high. More changed here than the ranged rotation suggests. The three trees are now three different ways to play, not three ways to amplify the same ranged pet base.

Every Hunter learns Aimed Shot at level twenty. Arcane Shot no longer shares a cooldown with it. Multi-Shot does instead. Traps work in combat, and fire and ice traps run on separate cooldowns, so you can keep one Fire Trap and one Ice Trap out at the same time. Traps become part of fighting, not just pull prep and Feign Death gymnastics.

Pets get one of the deepest redesigns. They inherit stats from your gear. Every family gets the basics, like Bite or Claw and Dash or Dive, and each family also gets its own utility ability. Blizzard keeps trimming the strongest ones. In the October sixteenth build, the Crocolisk's healing reduction was cut from fifty percent to twenty-five, the Bat's casting slow was halved, and the Bird of Prey's ability was restrained. So family choice matters more than in Vanilla, but don't count on early beta pet power surviving.

Beast Mastery gets Focused Fire, and at sixteen points, Summon Hawk, a temporary second attacker tied to your target. It's not modern Beast Mastery's zoo, but there's more going on than a bigger pet auto attack.

Marksmanship gives you a real petless path. Lone Wolf gives a large damage bonus when you play without a pet. Trueshot Aura moves deeper and becomes raid-wide. And the capstone, Sniper Shot, is a long cast, extreme range precision shot. If you only kept a pet because Vanilla forced you to, this spec lets you leave it behind.

Survival is the dramatic one. It's now an intentional melee and ranged hybrid. Predator's Edge boosts melee crit damage and off-hand play. Expose Prey lets attacks on your marked target enable Mongoose Bite. Strider Kick is a weapon attack tied to movement. Lacerating Strikes turns repeated Mongoose Bites into a bleed. And Resourcefulness folds traps and crits from both ranges into the spec. Importantly, the Classic dead zone remains. Survival is designed to move through the ranges with useful actions on both sides, not to erase range management.

One more detail from the October sixteenth build. There's now an option to switch automatically between auto attack and auto shot. Auto Shot still needs you to stand still for half a second, but it can fire during Aimed Shot and Sniper Shot casts. Shot timing remains part of the class.

The verdict: a Hunter that still follows old-school Hunter rules, but with three genuinely different fantasies. Survival in particular is close to a brand new spec.

## Rogue

Rogue. Distance from Vanilla: moderate. Classic Rogue with clearer specs, not a reinvention. Energy, combo points, positional attacks, poisons, stealth and control are all kept.

The baseline quality of life is real, though. Poisons carry more charges and stack with sharpening and weight stones. Sap no longer breaks your stealth. Distract also lowers the enemy's stealth detection. Expose Armor now equals five Sunders and no longer blocks Warriors from applying Sunder Armor, which ends an old raid debuff fight. And Rogues can use one-handed axes.

Assassination changes the most. It becomes the explicit dagger and poison spec. Mutilate is the twenty-one point centerpiece. It hits with both daggers, does more to poisoned targets, and awards two combo points. The capstone, Venom, is a finisher that buffs your poison damage and application. Your play revolves around keeping Slice and Dice and Venom running, with poisons as the core of your damage.

Combat stays the most recognizably Classic tree. Hack and Slash merges several weapon specializations, so a raid weapon drop is rarely the wrong type. Precision applies more broadly, Backstab gets more support, and Flawless Execution lowers the energy cost of Eviscerate so finishers flow better.

Subtlety is still the stealth, setup and control tree, now with a clearer damage loop. Premeditation sits at sixteen points. Hemorrhage now boosts your own Rupture instead of being mainly a raid debuff. Cutthroat lets Backstab open up Ambush-like attacks in combat. And the capstone, Thousand Cuts, makes Rupture ticks lower the energy cost of Hemorrhage and Backstab.

Blizzard has also said it wants Rogue to have the highest, or among the highest, single-target damage in a stand-still fight, trading utility and range for raw melee damage. That's a design goal, not proof of launch balance. Don't pick Rogue because it's supposed to top the meters.

The verdict: Forever Rogue is for someone who already likes Classic Rogue and wants better-defined trees. It's not the class to pick if you want something radically new.

## Priest

Priest. Distance from Vanilla: high. Priest gets a lot of tools from later expansions, while keeping Classic mana management and cast speed. All three specs become much more complete.

The headline baseline change is that Fear Ward and Devouring Plague are no longer race-locked. Every Priest gets them, and every race still gets two racial Priest spells of its own. Divine Spirit and Shadow Word: Death are baseline. Prayer of Healing can target another party. Several Priests can keep Renew on the same target. And Fade drops more threat.

For the Alliance, Humans keep Feedback and Divine Grace style options. Dwarves keep Desperate Prayer, with Chastise style utility. Night Elves have Starshards and Elune's Grace. And new Gnome Priests get their own racial spells. So race still matters for flavor, but raids no longer need you to be a Dwarf for Fear Ward.

Late beta also changed the Shadow buttons. Devouring Plague's cooldown went from one minute down to twenty-four seconds. Shadow Word: Death went from twelve seconds up to eighteen.

Discipline is the biggest change from Vanilla. It used to be the tree that held Power Infusion, Inner Focus, mana efficiency and some shields. Now it's a shield healing and damage weaving spec. Penance, the twenty-one point spell, can damage an enemy or heal an ally. Soul Warding improves repeated shields. Renewed Hope keys off Weakened Soul. Divine Aegis turns critical direct heals into absorbs. And Power in Light links Smite and Penance to targets carrying Holy Fire or Shadow Word: Pain. So you weave damage in on purpose, not just when healing is quiet.

Holy gets Binding Heal at sixteen points. Litany of Light rewards switching between different heals instead of repeating one. The capstone is Prayer of Mending, bouncing between allies as you remember it. Lightwell is removed. Vanilla Holy had lots of good ranks but settled on the efficient few. Forever rewards using the whole healing kit.

Shadow fixes famous Vanilla limits without becoming modern Shadow. Shadowform allows more non-healing spells, and adds big mana efficiency and crit damage. Shadow Weaving is now a personal buff, so you're less likely to be brought just as the Warlocks' debuff Priest. Vampiric Embrace becomes an activated thirty second cooldown that can't be resisted. Devouring Contagion spreads Devouring Plague to two more targets. Early Demise gives Shadow Word: Death an execute role. And Dark Inspiration makes Mind Blast and fully channeled Mind Flays raise Spirit for your party, while Improved Mind Flay ramps damage with each tick. Your filler finally has a reason to finish the channel.

The verdict: pick Priest if you want to stay recognizably Classic, but feel that your spec matters every time you play. Discipline is the spec you'll most need to relearn.

## Shaman

Shaman. Distance from Vanilla: very high. For the Alliance, it's the most novel choice by definition, because you simply couldn't roll one in Vanilla. Forever adds Dwarf Shaman. And even if you know Horde Shaman well, this version has one of the longest quality of life lists in the game.

Totems now use a one second global cooldown instead of one and a half. Long buff totems last five minutes instead of two. There's a dedicated totem interface. Weapon imbues last much longer and stack with temporary weapon enchants. And Fire Nova is now a spell fired through your placed fire totem.

Totemic Recall refunds some mana. Totemic Projection moves your existing totems. And three presets, Call of the Elements, Call of the Spirits and Call of the Ancestors, drop a whole set of totems at once. That removes most of the old four-global totem piano.

As of the October sixteenth build, Grace of Air can run alongside Windfury or Flametongue, although those two still exclude each other. Grace of Air, Strength of Earth, Windfury and Flametongue are all raid-wide. Chain Lightning's mana cost dropped by about thirty-five percent. And Fire Nova traded some damage for a big mana cost cut, so it's a sustainable utility button rather than a scaling spike.

Elemental has a real casting cycle now. Lightning Bolt and Chain Lightning have faster base casts than in Vanilla. And this is a place where the late build matters. An earlier deep dive had removed Elemental Mastery and put Lava Burst deeper in the tree. The October sixteenth build reversed that. Lava Burst moved up to twenty-one points, and Elemental Mastery came back as the capstone. It's a three minute cooldown. For fifteen seconds it shortens Lightning Bolt and Lava Burst casts, and removes Chain Lightning's cooldown. Earthbound sits at sixteen points, built around Earthbind and roots. So Elemental now has a Flame Shock, Lava Burst, Lightning Bolt and Chain Lightning rotation, plus a burst window.

Enhancement may be even more interesting. Mental Dexterity and Mental Quickness style talents turn Intellect into attack power and spell power. Improved Ghost Wolf works better indoors and can become instant. Stormstrike moves all the way up to sixteen points, on an eight second cooldown, and its debuff boosts your next Lightning Bolt, Chain Lightning or Earth Shock. Then Maelstrom Weapon speeds those casts as you fight, potentially to instant. You keep the two-handed axe and mace fantasy. It's not Retail's dual-wield Enhancement. But melee and spellcasting are finally connected instead of sitting side by side. Spirit Weapons and Rockbiter give some threat and defense, but Blizzard frames that as off-tanking or small group tanking, not a fourth fully supported raid tank.

Restoration gets the tools that fix Vanilla's one-note Chain Heal identity. Water Shield restores mana, and can now trigger from any Shaman spell crit, not just healing crits. Mana Tide moves to sixteen points, with an extra rank added at level thirty-eight. And the capstone, Riptide, is an instant heal plus a heal over time that strengthens Chain Heal on that target. Resto becomes about setting up Chain Heal and juggling several spells, not just spamming it.

The verdict: for an Alliance veteran who wants something that still feels like old WoW, but that they literally couldn't play on Alliance before, Dwarf Shaman belongs high on the shortlist. You still place totems and think about them. You just stop spending half your globals rebuilding the same four.

## Mage

Mage. Distance from Vanilla: high. The same Classic control chassis, with much more developed damage specs.

Frostfire Bolt arrives at level forty. It counts as both Fire and Frost, takes whichever resistance and damage interactions are better, and exists to make Fire and Frost hybrid builds legitimate.

Blizzard is also going after the most degenerate mass control and farming patterns. Improved Blizzard slows less. Cone of Cold's large group control was toned down, though the October build restored its full control on the first four targets. Blizzard is happy for Mages to handle small packs, but not to dominate giant mob trains forever. And because rank one Frostbolt is now penalized, there's a new spell at level twenty-four, Frost Blast. It's a one and a half second Frost cast with a forty percent slow that benefits from your Frostbolt talents. It keeps the useful control of rank one Frostbolt, and closes the downranking loophole.

Arcane is almost unrecognizable. It's a real damage spec now. Arcane Blast is a one and a half second cast. Each cast grants spell damage equal to fifteen percent of your Intellect, stacking up to four times, and makes the next Arcane Blast cost more. The stacks last fifteen seconds from the first one, and the spell's raw damage was cut by forty percent as part of the redesign. So you ramp up a large spell power state, then decide how hard to push before the mana cost hurts. That's a proper Classic resource puzzle. Missile Barrage can now proc from Arcane Missiles itself, and moved much earlier. And the sixteen point talent, Arcane Wrath, is a long cast whose damage scales partly from your maximum mana. Your mana pool becomes an offensive stat.

Fire becomes much more proc driven. Heating Up builds toward faster Pyroblasts. Every Fire Blast adds a charge, and a Fire Blast crit adds two. Critical Mass reaches ten percent crit at five points. Fire Power adds damage for each of your own Fire effects on the target, up to twelve percent. And Ignite is now tracked per caster, which ends the old shared Ignite mess. Instead of spamming Fireball and hoping Ignite behaves, you stack your Fire effects on a target and turn crits into Pyroblasts.

Frost gets the most familiar later imports, Ice Lance and Fingers of Frost. Fingers lets your attacks treat the target as frozen for Shatter, and Ice Lance hits much harder on frozen targets. Late beta cut that bonus from three hundred percent to two hundred. Winter's Chill is completely redesigned. It's no longer a stacking crit debuff. Frostbolt, Frost Blast and empowered Ice Lances build stacks, and your next Cone of Cold consumes them for much more damage. Cone of Cold becomes the finisher of a combo, not just an emergency slow.

The verdict: Mage is a great pick if your complaint about Vanilla Mage was that the toolkit is amazing, but raiding becomes one button. Forever's answer isn't Retail Mage. It's Classic Mage with an engine for each spec. Arcane especially is basically a brand new specialization.

## Warlock

Warlock. Distance from Vanilla: high. Probably the cleanest three-way spec split among the pure casters.

The baseline change is subtle but big. Curse of Agony and Curse of Doom become Banes. Banes are a separate category from utility curses, so putting your damage over time on a target no longer replaces the group's utility curse. Curse of the Elements now covers more magic schools, and Curse of Shadow is removed as redundant.

Firestones and Spellstones become weapon imbues that stack with oils. Life Tap now scales with Spirit, which ties caster gear directly to your health to mana engine. Drain Soul generates soul shards more reliably during the channel. And demons inherit more of your stats.

Affliction becomes a real damage over time, crit and drain spec. Pandemic lets your dots and drains critically strike. Soul Siphon rewards stacking your Affliction effects on a target. And the capstone, Wrack, is a channeled Shadow filler that amplifies the dots already running. In late beta, a talent called Malediction was replaced by Grim Reach, which extends Affliction's range, and Shadow Mastery was doubled, now going up to ten percent Shadow damage. Deep Affliction finally has a reason to stay deep at endgame.

Demonology is particularly unusual. Demonic Sacrifice moves much earlier. Then the capstone, Demonic Pact, lets you keep one demon's sacrifice bonus while summoning and keeping another. That resolves the old Vanilla joke that the famous Demonology playstyle was killing your demon for a buff. Decimation adds a Soul Fire execute. Demonic Brand improves pet threat and damage. And with better pet scaling, Demonology is genuinely committed to the demon.

Destruction gets stronger Fire and Shadow interplay. Conflagrate arrives much earlier, at sixteen points. Shadow and Fire casts strengthen each other through new talents. Bane of Havoc marks a second target that takes part of the damage you deal elsewhere, adding deliberate two-target cleave. And the capstone, Incinerate, hits much harder on targets with Immolate, giving Fire Destruction a real filler.

The verdict: Warlock didn't need headline repairs like Paladin or Druid, because Vanilla Warlock already worked. But the specs are dramatically better defined. If you love the Classic Warlock toolbox, but dislike how optimal builds blurred the trees together, Forever Warlock is a big improvement.

## Druid

Druid. Distance from Vanilla: very high. Perhaps the deepest Classic-plus redesign of them all. The changes reach the engine.

Most importantly, shapeshifted melee damage now scales with your weapon's damage per second, while forms keep fixed attack speeds. That ends the Vanilla assumption that a Feral weapon is just a stat stick, and that oddities like Manual Crowd Pummeler define the spec. Better weapons now mean better Cat and Bear damage.

Forms can use more objects, consumables and racials. Barkskin works while shifted. Entangling Roots works indoors. Nature's Grasp is baseline early. Omen of Clarity is baseline at level twenty. And Druids finally get an out of combat resurrection, called Revive. Bear gets Lacerate at level forty-two, for stacking bleed and threat, and Swipe scales with attack power. Forms are immune to Disarm once you're shifted, though a Disarm before you shift still hurts. And in the October sixteenth build, Furor lets you keep your Cat energy when you briefly shift through Bear, although energy doesn't regenerate while you're a Bear.

Balance is built around a new Eclipse, but not the modern bar. Casting Wrath shortens your next Starfires, which gives you a reason to alternate the two nukes. Insect Swarm sits at sixteen points. Nature's Splendor extends your effects, and Moonkin Form allows more non-healing spells. Moonglow helps mana efficiency, and Moonkin Aura is now raid-wide spell crit. Balance is no longer a Starfire caster with chronic mana problems and a novelty aura. It's still slow cast Classic Balance, with no Retail Astral Power.

Feral is the most fascinating change if you understand Classic powershifting. Tiger's Fury is gone. It's replaced by Shifting Power, which converts a chunk of base mana into energy on a cooldown. Mana stays a secondary resource you spend for Cat damage, but as a deliberate talent, not by canceling and re-entering Cat Form with a Wolfshead Helm and Furor. Claw, Shred and Rake scale better. Primal Bite becomes a key attack. And Berserk, deep in the tree, removes Primal Bite's cooldown, makes it hit several enemies, and greatly raises the crit chance of your combo point builders. The rhythm itself changes. Blizzard took powershifting's core idea and made it supported design.

Restoration gets the most recognizable imports. Swiftmend moves to sixteen points, and no longer consumes the heal over time it uses. That's a huge usability win. The capstone is Wild Growth, healing several party members over time. Gift of the Earthmother speeds up the global cooldown on Rejuvenation, Swiftmend and Wild Growth. Mark of the Wild upgrades no longer eat premium talent points, and Spirit regeneration is better. Resto keeps its Classic heal over time and Healing Touch identity, and gains a real instant reactive heal and a sustained group heal.

The verdict: if you know Classic deeply, you'll notice Druid's changes constantly. Weapon upgrades matter in forms. Powershifting is formalized. Bear has better threat tools. Balance has a spell cycle. And Resto gets core healing tools. It's still Vanilla Druid, minus a remarkable number of its structural oddities.

## Choosing your class

So how should a veteran choose?

If you want the most improved version of a class you already know, start with Paladin, Druid, Shaman, Hunter, Priest and Mage. In those six, the new design changes what you do often enough that you'll keep thinking, oh, this is Forever.

If you want Vanilla feel with the rough edges sanded off, Warrior and Rogue are your best bets. Warrior still revolves around rage, swings, stances and threat. Rogue still revolves around energy, combo points, poisons, positioning and stealth.

If you hated the Vanilla hybrid tax, look at Paladin and Druid. Prot Paladin finally has a taunt and area tanking. Feral gets weapon scaling and supported energy conversion. Balance gets a spell loop. And Ret becomes an intentionally supported damage spec.

If you want the single most novel Alliance experience, Dwarf Shaman is hard to beat. A whole class Vanilla Alliance never had, and a much richer one than the Horde original.

If you always wished Survival Hunter meant the Hunter who fights up close, Forever finally does that. And with dual spec, you can still keep a petless Marksmanship or a pet-heavy Beast Mastery setup on the same character.

If you love Priest but found the healing trees mechanically samey, Discipline may be the sleeper pick.

If your Mage complaint was raid boredom rather than class weakness, the Arcane, Fire and Frost engines give you much more to think about, without deleting Blink, Polymorph, Counterspell or Frost Nova.

And if you love the Warlock toolbox but hated how endgame builds blurred the trees, Forever Warlock gives you three real specs.

If I had to rank them purely on how much Forever changes the experience: Shaman is top tier for novelty. Paladin is top tier for a repaired fantasy. Druid is top tier for mechanical depth. Hunter is the top pure damage dealer refresh. Priest and Mage are excellent caster refreshes. Warlock is excellent but more evolutionary. And Warrior and Rogue are the Classic-plus choices. That is not a power ranking. Final raid damage, the tank hierarchy and healing throughput are exactly the things most likely to move. Blizzard was still changing coefficients, talent positions, raid buffs and cooldowns in mid October.

## Closing

Here's the one idea to take away. Don't ask which Vanilla class you already like most. Ask which one had a fantasy you loved, but mechanics you wished Blizzard had finished. Forever's class work is aimed squarely at that second question.

Paladin, Druid and Shaman are the most transformed. Hunter, Priest, Mage and Warlock gain distinct spec loops. Warrior and Rogue stay closest to refined Classic originals.

That's the Forever Class Codex. See you in Azeroth on November fourth.
